What happens to mathematics when AI becomes an oracle?
Our modern world runs on math, from social media algorithms, package delivery, and weather prediction to medical imaging and machine learning. The rise of computers helped fuel its dominance, enabling us to work with increasingly large numbers that would otherwise be beyond the capacity of our finite human brains. But with the rise of large language models, AI chatbots, and, soon, quantum computing, so-called “big math” is beginning to outstrip its creators entirely. What that means , and how we might adapt, is the focus of a new book, Big Math: The Hidden Codes That Run Our World , by applied mathematicians Steven Strogatz and Alex Townsend. Strogatz is perhaps best known in his field for his co-authorship of a seminal 1998 paper with Duncan Watts on the collective dynamics of small-world networks. He has also studied chaos theory as applied to physics, the geometry of supercoiled DNA, and the biological collective oscillators, such as swarms of synchronously flashing fireflies and crickets that chirp in unison. In 2005, Strogatz extended his study of synchronicity to the crowds that caused the wobbling of London’s Millennium Bridge . An award-winning communicator, Strogatz wrote three series of columns on mathematics for The New York Times in 2010, 2012, and 2025 and hosted a podcast for Quanta magazine from 2020–2021. He has written four popular books: Sync (2003), The Calculus of Friendship (2009), The Joy of X (2012), and the New York Times bestseller Infinite Powers (2019). This time around, Strogatz has found a well-matched co-author in Townsend, a younger colleague recently hired at Cornell who studies numerical analysis and deep learning. As senior faculty, Strogatz was assigned to mentor Townsend, and the two men began regularly lunching together in Risley Hall. Strogatz was intrigued by the popular book potential of Townsend’s course on the top 20 algorithms of the 20th century and suggested co-authorship. That concept slowly morphed into a book about big matrix calculations, and then into what became Big Math . “There are so many books about pure math, as if computers didn’t exist,” Strogatz told Ars. “Alex and I are both very applied mathematicians. We thought there should be a book on computers and math, especially the class of really big problems with billions of variables or parameters that you just can’t approach in your head. A lot of the math we’ve had for centuries perfectly scales up to this computer moment. What the abacus did for arithmetic, the fangcheng (“rectangular array”) board did for systems of linear equations. It was a matrix you could manipulate with your hand and bamboo sticks. So this externalizing of math outside of our heads into our tools is a 2,000-year-old idea.” “The mathematician in each of us will always want to know more,” Strogatz and Townsend wrote. “The human being must insist that knowledge serves something larger than itself. That might be the work of our century: letting our power grow without losing our soul. Big Math keeps getting bigger. So must we.” Ars caught up with Strogatz to learn more. Ars Technica: Let’s start by defining “big math,” because it’s such a broad umbrella term. Steven Strogatz : Operationally, big math is “big” relative to human cognitive capacity. So it can be surprisingly small and yet big. We also wanted to expand it to things that people would think of as computational engineering—in the building of the Burj Khalifa, that’d be civil engineering or design architecture. It’s there in medical imaging. It’s in getting us to the Moon. The lunar module was controlled by a tiny computer that only had to think about 20 or 30 variables at a time to steer the LEM down to the Moon’s surface. But it’s not something a person could do in their head. Big Math is not simply big in the ordinary sense of big numbers. There’s an undercurrent in the book about the importance of linearity and sparsity. When problems are linear, they can be scaled up to immense size, and they stay tractable. If they’re non-linear, you don’t have any hope of dealing with them with pure math. You can do them with computers. But for the people who want rigorous theorems, we’re in terra incognita when we deal with anything that’s non-linear and big. Small math can be very hard. Historically, we had small math problems with one or two variables, arithmetic, algebra that you learn in high school. Even to this day, a lot of pure math is relatively small. It doesn’t concern itself with enormous numbers of variables simultaneously because it’s totally intractable. Cryptography can be small, but it’s so complicated with its non-linearities and other algebraic difficulties. So big is one way to be difficult, but there are a lot of other ways. Nowadays, trillions of parameters are typical in the biggest of the large language models. But infinity is not big from this point. Infinity, which you would think of would be the biggest of big math, is not because calculus fits perfectly nicely in a human head. Infinity turns out to be very tractable in a way that a billion is not. Ars: One of your real-world examples of big math is particularly relevant at the moment: mathematics as it relates to the gerrymandering issue. Strogatz : It’s an interesting question of, what’s a fair map and what’s a reasonable redistricting? The legal system gives us certain criteria, but they are vague. The districts should not be too snakey-looking, not look like salamanders, for instance. The term in the law is ‘compact.’ They should be reasonably compact. But what does that mean mathematically ? The districts are also supposed to be contiguous; you don’t want to have separate islands and call that one district. It’s supposed to respect natural boundaries like rivers or other landscape features. Also, each district is supposed to have roughly the same population. So how to make maps that respect all of the criteria? It turns out there are a lot of ways to make them.
