Astra can read and write weights to improve Qwen
We find that frontier LLMs are surprisingly good at natively reading and writing neural network weights , well past what we’d expect from humans or conventional algorithms. This might be a big deal for both optimization and interpretability. Just by looking at the raw weights, GPT-6 Astra can tell what a network was trained to do . And without any environment feedback, it has no trouble writing, from scratch, small game-playing networks that outplay networks trained with PPO for 100 million steps. Likewise, asked to make Qwen3.5-0.8B better at math without gradients, Astra identifies an issue in its weights causing reasoning loops that Qwen’s own model card warns about, and fixes this by natively editing 0.016% of the weights . With the same sampling settings, this weight correction raises MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1% and generalizes out of the box to instruction following, raising IFEval by 3x. All of this is possible without allowing Astra to write training loops. It uses its reasoning to figure out what each part of a neural network computes, or should compute, and changes the weights accordingly. We call this Weight-Space Programming . Throughout the history of deep learning, we encoded our simplistic inductive biases into tiny symbolic optimizers like Adam or Muon. This is changing: our neural network weights will soon be natively optimized by LLMs that deliberately reason about their weights . If you were asked to handcraft a neural network that plays Go, what would you do? If you were handed raw neural network weights and asked to predict their behavior, where would you even start? For almost any human, this is practically impossible. Today, weights are written by optimization , by custom networks trained to write them , or by compilers from formal programs . In this post, we find that the current generation of frontier LLMs, used natively in natural language, are starting to become eerily good at understanding and crafting neural network weights around natural-language goals. For a few years now, we’ve all been using LLMs to optimize text artifacts like prompts and the programs built around them . More recently, we’ve started using them to optimize whole harnesses and the training code and hyperparameters that train ML models . We often justified these methods by reference to the fact that we don’t know other principled ways to hill-climb on text artifacts, unlike for updating the weights of differentiable neural networks . After all, humans can “optimize” code by hand, for example, so it made sense to ask LLMs to do it, but they can’t “optimize” neural weights by hand, so those remained largely in the realm of symbolic algorithms. In this post, we share very early results that allow us to argue that the paradigm of reasoning-driven optimization is now coming even for differentiable neural networks . In Weight-Space Programming , a general-purpose LLM reads and writes a network’s weights directly (i.e., not through an optimizer, and in particular not with gradient descent) around some natural language spec, sometimes even with no environment feedback at all. In natural language, it’s becoming possible to “just ask” an LLM to natively find a hidden trait or failure inside the weights of a neural network, to fix that failure, or to write a new network from scratch . We report early, exciting results on each of these below, first on small networks and then even on real language models: We start with a small-scale experiment to see if LLMs can understand the continuous weight space, which is essentially just a list of numbers. In our first experiments, we train some neural network with some algorithm. It would be extremely exciting if the LLM is able to tell its behavior just from the weights. We trained neural network policies with PPO to play four computer games. All four networks take the same input channels, choose from the same actions, and have exactly the same network dimensions, so that it is not able to tell unless the weights are read.
