The fight over Flock and other ALPRs
There are over 120,000 of Flock’s automatic license plate reader (ALPR) cameras installed all over the US. Flock’s cameras, and others like them, use AI to identify and track vehicles based on their license plate number, make, model, color, and other info, networked together to track vehicles and people’s movements throughout the day and across the country. Dozens of communities have canceled contracts for the cameras or turned them off, including Los Angeles , as officials have pushed for more limits on the data they collect, who has access to it, and how it can be used in the future. Earlier this year, Amazon-owned Ring canceled a partnership with Flock after online backlash. Surveillance tech company Flock is rolling out updates to address reports of cops across the country abusing its tools to stalk ex-romantic partners and others. CEO Garrett Langley is delivering a mea culpa, and in an interview with The Verge , says he’s changed his mind on what responsibility Flock bears for how law enforcement uses it. Over the coming weeks, Flock will begin implementing updates that include requiring a previously optional feature called Audit Assistance, which flags “abnormal search behavior” and immediately locks out suspicious users until an administrator reviews their activity. All users will also be required to enter case codes to run searches, ideally limiting the ability to run a query for personal or nefarious purposes. This can be overridden by an administrator for emergency situations, like tracking a missing child, but those will automatically be flagged for review. As BoingBoing notes , a Tennessee candidate for Congress was charged for four counts of felony vandalism for allegedly shooting up Flock cameras. The Blount County sheriff’s office says the suspect admitted to shooting the “ALPR systems” over about a week in July. To say these systems are unpopular is becoming an understatement. Joel Feder at The Drive reported a frightening story about how Flock cameras , recently suspended by the LAPD, tracked him in Minnesota and sent police after him over “stolen plates.” (They were not.) Flock says its cameras worked “correctly” in the lead-up to a potentially deadly incident. But here we are in our new era of computer policing . Over the weekend, the three-year deal for Flock Safety’s 138 pole-mounted surveillance cameras came to an end, as the LA Times reports the LAPD CIO says it ended the arrangement while working on a new contract that has “very clear terms” about who owns the data collected and what happens to it . An unnamed spokesperson for Flock told media outlets that unspecified “misconceptions” led to the pause. On Thursday, the Instagram account for a lecture series in Newport Beach, CA posted a photo of what appeared to be a cease and desist letter from the surveillance technology company Flock Safety. Flock has received significant backlash over its technology and work with law enforcement agencies, and this letter kicked off yet another wave of criticism against the company for allegedly trying to shut down discussions of its work. “WE WILL NOT BE SILENCED,” the group, The Saturday Salon, wrote in the Instagram post , which has more than 3,000 likes. A separate post about the letter on Bluesky has more than 360 reposts . The text of the letter demands that the group stop hosting conversations about Flock’s surveillance technology. In a DM from The Saturday Salon’s Instagram account, Schuyler Lifschultz tells The Verge that it “found this letter taped to our front door.” A report from 404 Media shows how more than a dozen cases around the US have shown police using Flock to illegally stalk victims . Flock acknowledged it’s aware of 15 incidents, claiming“each surfaced because of the transparency and accountability features deliberately built into our platform.” But that doesn’t reflect how some victims only found out by using the HaveIBeenFlocked.com website that Flock has tried to have taken down, or the many years of similar reports with other surveillance tech, a problem the NSA tagged “ LOVEINT .” Today, let’s talk about the camera company Ring, lost dogs, and the surveillance state. 为Flock和其他ALPR而战
Flock的自动车牌阅读器（ALPR）摄像头在美国各地安装了超过12万个。Flock的摄像头和其他类似的摄像头使用人工智能根据车牌号、品牌、型号、颜色和其他信息识别和跟踪车辆，并将其联网，以跟踪车辆和人们在一天中和全国各地的行动。包括洛杉矶在内的数十个社区已经取消或关闭了摄像头的合同，因为官员们要求对他们收集的数据、谁可以访问这些数据以及未来如何使用这些数据进行更多限制。今年早些时候，亚马逊旗下的Ring在网上遭到强烈反对后取消了与Flock的合作关系。监控技术公司Flock正在推出更新，以解决全国各地警察滥用其工具跟踪前恋人和其他人的报道。首席执行官Garrett Langley正在道歉，并在接受The Verge采访时表示，他已经改变了对Flock对执法部门如何使用Flock的责任的看法。在未来几周内，Flock将开始实施更新，包括要求一个以前可选的功能，称为审计协助，该功能标记“异常搜索行为”，并立即锁定可疑用户，直到管理员审查他们的活动。所有用户还将被要求输入案例代码来运行搜索，理想情况下会限制出于个人或恶意目的运行查询的能力。这可以由管理员在紧急情况下覆盖，例如跟踪失踪儿童，但这些情况会自动标记以供审查。正如BoingBoing所指出的那样，田纳西州的一名国会候选人因涉嫌枪杀Flock相机而被指控犯有四项重罪故意破坏罪。布朗特县警长办公室表示，嫌疑人承认在7月的大约一周内拍摄了“ALPR系统”。说这些系统不受欢迎是一种轻描淡写。The Drive的Joel Feder报道了一个可怕的故事，讲述了最近被洛杉矶警察局暂停的Flock摄像头如何在明尼苏达州追踪他，并派警察追捕他“偷来的车牌”。（事实并非如此。）Flock表示，在可能致命的事件发生之前，其摄像头“正常”工作。但我们正处于计算机监管的新时代。上周末，Flock Safety的138个安装在杆上的监控摄像头的三年协议结束了，据《洛杉矶时报》报道，洛杉矶警察局首席信息官表示，他们在制定一份新合同的同时终止了这一安排，该合同对收集的数据的所有权和处理方式有“非常明确的条款”。Flock的一位未透露姓名的发言人告诉媒体，未指明的“误解”导致了暂停。周四，加利福尼亚州纽波特比奇一系列讲座的Instagram账户发布了一张照片，照片上似乎是监控技术公司Flock Safety的停止函。Flock的技术和与执法机构的合作遭到了强烈反对，这封信引发了另一波针对该公司的批评，据称该公司试图阻止对其工作的讨论。“我们不会沉默，”该组织“周六沙龙”在Instagram帖子中写道，该帖子有3000多个赞。关于这封信的另一篇帖子在Bluesky上被转发了360多次。信中要求该组织停止主持有关Flock监控技术的对话。在《周六沙龙》Instagram账户的DM中，Schuyler Lifschultz告诉The Verge，它“发现这封信贴在了我们的前门”。404媒体的一篇报道显示，美国各地的十几起案件显示，警方利用Flock非法跟踪受害者。Flock承认它知道15起事件，声称“每一起事件都是因为我们平台中故意内置的透明度和问责制功能而浮出水面的。”但这并不能反映一些受害者是如何通过使用Flock试图删除的HaveIBeenFlocked.com网站，或者多年来其他监控技术的类似报告才发现的，美国国家安全局将这个问题标记为“LOVEINT”。今天，让我们谈谈相机公司Ring、丢失的狗和监控状态。你可能在几个周末前的超级碗期间看到过这则广告：Ring关于与Flock Safety分道扬镳的声明中最引人注目的是这家家庭安全公司没有说什么。没有提到公众对与ICE关系的强烈反对，也没有提到任何解决用户对该公司与执法部门关系的担忧的承诺。
