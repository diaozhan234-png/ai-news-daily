Traumatized teens sue school, cops after Doritos bag triggers AI gun alert
In October 2025, four Black high school students were sharing Cool Ranch Doritos after football practice, winding down and waiting for their rides home. It was then, in the lull of a moment any teen with after-school activities knows well, that the school’s AI gun detection system suddenly flagged one teen holding a bag of chips as a potential gunman. Within two minutes, Baltimore County Public Schools officials who reviewed security camera footage linked to the alert determined there was no actual threat. They recognized the Doritos bag and canceled the alert, which notified not only other school officials but also the Baltimore County Police Department that no response was needed. The false alert could have ended there—before a traumatizing campus clash with police—but it didn’t. According to a lawsuit filed by the current and former students, Taki Allen, Yui-Osa Ogbeide, Devon Thompson Jr., and Keith Winfield III, due to “both technological and human errors,” school officials called police to the scene anyway, and the cops responded with force. At least six patrol cars arrived with lights flashing, and some cops emerged with guns drawn. The teens froze and cooperated, confused and scared for their lives, their lawsuit said. “No one told the students what they were suspected of,” the lawsuit said. “Officers separated the students, ordered them to their knees on the concrete, handcuffed them, and searched their persons and property.” Only after the cops failed to find a gun did students learn that it was the school’s AI gun detection system, Omnilert, that triggered the police response. Allegedly, one of the responding officers muttered, “stupid AI cameras” and “fucking Omnilerts,” while another told the students that “AI is not the best.” Finally releasing the students, one officer seemed to acknowledge that they had put the teens in danger. “Hey, thanks for not running away, that would have been a real problem,” the officer allegedly said. Walking away, another joked that the boys should remember to “be careful with your chip bags.” The cops did not just target the suspected gunman but apparently told the other teens that they were guilty “by association” when they asked why they were searched. Suing to stop any other teens from facing allegedly life-or-death situations due to officials’ blind trust of AI, the teens have asked for an injunction barring BCPS from using Omnilert until they can effectively establish protocols to prevent police responses to false alerts. The lawsuit alleged that students’ constitutional rights were violated, as students were unlawfully searched and detained. Damon Hewitt, president and executive director of the Lawyers’ Committee for Civil Rights Under Law—a nonpartisan nonprofit that’s representing the teens in the suit—said that “overreliance on artificial intelligence technology mixed with inept communication and coordination among key officials is a toxic mix.” The complaint noted that BCPS was among the earliest adopters of Omnilert, and officials have touted it as “the largest deployment of AI visual gun detection” as a means to supposedly protect students. Instead, they alleged that Omnilert is marketing a defective product as triggering “near-zero false positives” without effectively requiring the adequate human review that claim depends on. “We need a reckoning on how AI is being used, especially concerning public safety and kids,” Hewitt said. “We are fighting to deliver justice in this case, but it also holds lessons for educators and police officials across the country who should think twice before putting our children’s fate in the hands of AI.” All the teens feel less safe on campus and less trusting of police following the incident. They also suffered from physical pain—including ”deep red marks” on their wrists from handcuffs—as well as “acute and lasting emotional distress, fear, anxiety,” and “humiliation.” Further, the teens report that rumors follow them around, falsely claiming that there was a gun and damaging their reputations. Car pick-up will never be the same. The student falsely flagged as the gunman, Taki Allen, “no longer feels safe waiting for rides or spending time outside the school after hours” and “frequently pays for rides home from private car services to avoid waiting for rides from friends or family.” Keith Winfield’s house was very close to the school, but his mother, Frieda Winfield, said his anxiety was so bad that he would call her “multiple times after football practice ended to confirm that she was on her way to pick him up.” Frieda Winfield called for change following her son Keith’s chilling interaction. “The police told my son this could have been a real problem,” Frieda Winfield said. “No child in Baltimore County should face this kind of danger while waiting outside his own school for a ride home from his mother.” The teens’ lawsuit targets Omnilert and responding Baltimore County Police Department officers. It also challenges Baltimore County Public Schools and individual school officials who allegedly failed to establish proper protocols to prevent a police response to a canceled alert. In a later investigation, the Office of the Inspector General (OIG) for Education concluded that Omnilert’s system had no “bias.” But OIG acknowledged that the “delegation of authority” process that Kenwood High School set up didn’t stop its principal, Kate Smith, from noticing the alert, failing to see it was canceled, and texting a school resource officer to deploy police 15 minutes later—all while acknowledging in that text that the object in the alert was “maybe not” a gun. Smith apparently was never trained on the Omnilert system, OIG’s report indicated. The OIG agreed with teens that things should change. Immediately after the incident, an internal Omnilert training link was shared with BCPS staff, and OIG recommended that schools conduct biannual trainings moving forward.
