Review: The Asus ProArt P16 offers a glimpse of a future powered by local AI
Buying a new computer in the last few years has meant wading through all manner of AI branding nonsense . Most of the AI pitches for laptops are little more than marketing hype, but the launch of Nvidia RTX Spark could bring a meaningful improvement in AI capabilities to the device you use every day. Asus has adopted Nvidia’s new chip in its latest ProArt lineup, offering a staggering amount of memory to run local AI models. While it doesn’t have a traditional GPU, the Asus ProArt P16 , with up to 128GB of memory, can also be your primary gaming and productivity machine, but this unified experience comes at a high price. If you want to run a small army of agents on your laptop, you’re looking at $7,000 for the top-end ProArt P16, and despite being more approachable on Windows, local AI still requires a lot of trial and error. Before we dive into the AI agents, let’s talk about the ProArt P16 as a laptop. Nvidia’s pitch is that the RTX Spark can be your one true machine for AI, productivity, and gaming. And it doesn’t skimp on the specs. Asus has released numerous ProArt laptops, but this one is notable for including the new Nvidia Spark RTX chip in its most powerful configuration. The Spark N1X has a 20-core Grace CPU, divided between 10 high-performance Arm Cortex-X925 cores and 10 power-efficient Arm Cortex-A725 cores. The performance cluster reaches boost speeds between 4.0 and 4.1 GHz, while the efficiency cores peak at 2.8 GHz. Memory caching on the processor totals 49MB, split between 24MB of shared L3 cache and 25MB of L2 cache. Graphics and AI are the purview of the Nvidia GB20B Blackwell GPU. It packs 6,144 cores in this configuration across 48 streaming multiprocessors, backed by its own 24MB L2 cache. These 5th-generation Tensor Cores support the full suite of Nvidia technologies, including DLSS, Reflex, ACE, G-Sync, CUDA, and NVFP4. In all, you’re looking at up to 31 TFLOPS of FP32 graphical performance and about 1,000 TOPS of FP4 AI compute. Asus will sell this device in two memory configurations (plus some lower-specced retailer-specific SKUs), but the one I’ve been testing (and the one that makes the most sense alongside the RTX Spark) has 128GB of LPDDR5x RAM. It supports up to 300GB/s of raw bandwidth, which is fast for shared system memory. However, using a dedicated GPU typically gets you higher throughput with a GDDR7 memory interface. Still, you get several times more memory than a gaming GPU in this machine. This is essentially an Nvidia Spark DGX workstation with a screen and keyboard. It also runs Windows 11, which lowers the barrier to entry if you want local AI agents. While many local AI setups focus on 27-billion-parameter models that will fit on a video card, the Asus ProArt P16 can easily load models with 100-plus billion parameters, with enough left over for lighter background AI tasks. Nvidia and Asus say the Spark’s AI compute and ample memory are enough power to run frontier-level AI models locally. That means you can get powerful AI compute without running through your allotment of expensive tokens or sending all your data to a server in the cloud. Ignoring the AI angle, the ProArt P16 has to be a good laptop before it makes sense for even the most AI-obsessed buyers to consider it. Luckily, it gets all the basic laptop things right. It’s nice to see a wide array of ports on this machine. There are three USB-C ports with USB4 support, and they’re split between the left and right sides. That makes connecting devices and charging much easier. There’s also a legacy USB-A 3.2 Gen 2 port, full HDMI, a 3.5 mm jack, and an SD card reader. The machine comes with a beefy 140 W USB-C charger, which can get you a few hours of use in just a minute or two of charging. Asus says you can get up to 12 hours of use per charge, but that depends very much on what you’re doing. Watching video at low brightness might get you even more than 12 hours, but expect somewhat less for mixed use and even less for heavy AI workloads. In my (unscientific) testing, after a full workday and tinkering with AI on the side, the P16 usually has about 30 percent of its battery left. The 16-inch 120Hz OLED display is overall very good, sporting a 4K+ resolution of 3840 x 2400. The stacked (tandem) pixels mean you get an incredibly high 1,600 nits of peak brightness. However, tandem OLEDs do tend to look a bit grainy with solid colors at lower brightness levels. I’ve laid eyes on two P16 units, one of which was noticeably more grainy than the other—so that’s something to look out for. However, the texture is easy to ignore at normal viewing distances and mid-to-high brightness. The trackpad is enormous (150.3 x 99.5 mm) and relies on haptic clicks instead of a physical mechanism. This is a relatively new thing for Windows laptops, which are still catching up to Apple’s trackpads. That said, the ProArt’s pad works very well. It’s appropriately sensitive, and you can adjust the click intensity and detection threshold. The keyboard is a little on the narrow side for a larger laptop, but the keys feel great. There’s ample travel, the tactility is sharp, and the firm keyboard deck ensures no flex at the bottom of each keystroke. The aluminum chassis is overall very solid, but it only comes in white or black, with the latter being your only option for the high-end model. The matte case looks great, but it is a magnet for smudges (particularly the plastic of the keys). Unlike previous ProArt laptops that had standard GPUs, there’s no dedicated memory for the RTX Spark’s graphics and AI cores. Windows sees the machine as having 64GB of system RAM and 64GB of video memory, but in reality, this shared 128GB of memory is adjusted dynamically based on what you’re doing. Since this is a Windows machine, there is some overhead that limits your AI workloads. On a DGX Spark box with the same underlying hardware, all the memory is available for AI. Here, you’re capped at about 110GB.
