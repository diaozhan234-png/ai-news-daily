Neural networks are clean
The cause of polysemanticity in early model organisms wasn’t superposition, but several ordinary, mostly fixable issues. We can now understand most neurons in models up to trillions of parameters, and think the outlook for mechanistic interpretability may be far better than we thought. A working hypothesis · 65-minute read · Text version I think I have good news about the nature of neural networks. After many months studying neurons and circuits in image and language models, I’ve come to believe a network’s own mechanisms are its legible building blocks, and that the outlook for mechanistic interpretability may be far better than I’d feared. The canonical evidence of polysemanticity and superposition that convinced much of the field, including me, that networks pack concepts arbitrarily was misread. What looked like arbitrary packing was mostly a motif I’ll call recursive generalization, which is much less concerning. This means we can study networks directly, instead of first untangling them. Neural networks are already clean. There are at least two ways to approach mechanistic interpretability. The first is rapid normal science on the network itself: look at a network’s neurons and circuits, study how they relate, watch data flow through them, and run experiments at agentic scale to test explanations. The second starts from a list of hypothesized features and tries to extract them all, designing increasingly sophisticated reconstruction objectives and usually ignoring the original. The field began with the first approach and fell back to the second because neural networks seemed to be tangled knots. We had canonical examples of polysemantic neurons, the broad name for a neuron that appears to have multiple unrelated jobs or that looks impossible to understand. The most famous one, in an image model, responded to both cat faces and the fronts of cars. And the last layer of the early field’s model organism, InceptionV1, seemed so full of arbitrary mixtures it wasn’t clear how to study it: almost none of the neurons were easily interpretable. The leading explanation for polysemanticity was superposition. This proposed a new learning principle: when a neural network runs out of neurons, it stops grouping related things together and instead begins folding unrelated things together, simply because they don’t interfere. The result is a kind of knot: the network could have folded in many almost equally good ways, so which things a neuron groups together tells you almost nothing. You have to unfold the knot before you can study anything. In practice that means training a second model – a decomposition such as a sparse autoencoder – to approximate the network as it would look unfolded and studying that instead. If the network really is knotted, there may be no alternative. One main result is that superposition was the wrong explanation for InceptionV1. Architectural problems made later layers overflow. Weak interpretability tools amplified these problems, making the late layers seem incoherent. Since then, architectural changes designed to help deep layers learn better have had the unintended effect of making them more interpretable. When we first studied InceptionV1, we found almost no interpretable neurons in its last layer. But with these ordinary improvements, and no change to model size or training data, 99-100% of neurons in that last layer are easily interpretable and the model is broadly legible the whole way through. It’s the first non-trivial neural network I feel I mostly understand; you can browse it here . I trained versions of it across a 300× range in size, and it’s broadly legible at every scale. With less room, the network doesn’t fold unrelated things together; its neurons just cover coarser-grained concepts, like one car neuron covering many kinds of car. With more room, that neuron differentiates into many specific cars. So the problem wasn’t the presence of a second learning principle, it was just an ordinary learning failure of the first one, and it’s easy to fix.
