Google's AI answers reducing search traffic by 40%
Google's AI answers are taking clicks from websites in the search results. In a randomized experiment, researchers hid AI Overviews from some US desktop users. Comparing them with users who still saw Overviews shows that an Overview cuts clicks to outside websites by about 40%. Those Overviews now appear on more than half the US keywords DataForSEO tracks, up from 6% two years ago. The industry's answer, GEO (generative engine optimization), has yet to show a way to compensate for the loss. While AI citations correlate with the same kind of link profile that goes with higher Google rankings, in March AI assistants sent websites just 0.3% of their traffic. Search engine optimization (SEO) still decides whether you can be found, but where an AI answer appears, a ranking brings 40% fewer visits than it used to. Generative engine optimization (GEO), the practice of getting your content cited in AI-written answers, looks mostly like SEO plus two additions: being present on the third-party sites AI answers draw from, and writing pages that are easy to quote. We found little evidence for any other tactic. An AI Overview is a generated summary that Google places above the ordinary results, with links to the pages it drew on. In DataForSEO's monthly panel of 20 million keywords, the share of results showing one climbed from 5.93% in September 2024 to 61.43% in August 2026. The chart at the top of this post shows that climb. Over the same period the featured snippet, the quoted passage Google used to show at the top of a page, fell from 8.96% to 0.44%, and the discussions and forums block fell from 21.13% to 7.42%. Video results rose from 9.67% to 19.98%, though that is down from a peak of 31.05% in October 2025, and local map listings rose from 19.98% to 25.99%. DataForSEO's dataset covers 190 million keywords, 165 million AI answers, and 1.1 billion backlinks, mostly for US English searches. Pew Research Center's data gives an independent check. Pew logged the searches of 900 US adults in March 2025, re-ran them in April, and found summaries on 18% of them. DataForSEO's figures for those months are 14.67% and 17.63%, close to Pew's. You can't reliably avoid Overviews by choosing different keywords. Between the fourth quarter of 2025 and the third quarter of 2026, the rate rose for every type of search intent: Low-volume keywords aren't safer either. In the third quarter of 2026, keywords with 201 to 300 monthly searches showed Overviews 58.59% of the time, against 48.62% for those above 600. A separate dataset, from June to September 2026, covers difficulty and price. The easiest keywords showed Overviews 60.64% of the time and the hardest 53.76%. Keywords costing advertisers more than $5 a click were at 49.55% and those under 25 cents at 50.54%, with a low of 42.63% between $0.51 and $1.00. More than half the result pages have no price data and show Overviews 60.06% of the time. The rate does vary by industry. Health is at 69.68% and finance at 68.80%, while apparel is at 37.48%, dining at 29.37%, and real estate at 22.27%. Queries are also getting longer, especially on desktop. Over a year, queries of five or more words grew from 29.3% to 34.8% of unique desktop queries and from 29.6% to 31.9% on mobile. Three and four word searches are still the most common. The strongest evidence is an experiment. Saharsh Agarwal and Ananya Sen gave US desktop Chrome users an extension that put each of them at random into a group. One group saw Google as usual, and another had AI Overviews hidden. The researchers then watched two weeks of real searches in January and February 2026. On searches that would show an Overview, the group that could see them averaged 0.37 clicks to outside websites per search, against 0.62 for the group that couldn't, which the authors report as a 39.8% reduction. Searches ending with no outbound click went from 54% to 73%. Clicks on ads didn't change, which suggests Google takes care not to cannibalize its own revenue. Across all searches, with and without an Overview, the authors put the loss at roughly 18.5% of the organic clicks a site gets from Google. The authors expect the loss to grow as Overviews appear on more searches. This is a randomized controlled trial, the most reliable kind of study for separating cause from correlation or coincidence. The effect was statistically significant for informational searches. The navigational and transactional samples were too small to settle anything. This is a working paper, not yet peer reviewed, and it covers desktop only. A separate preregistered experiment by Wang and colleagues, with 1,100 people, also found that removing AI Overviews and AI Mode raised clicks to publishers. Observational data points the same way. Pew found people clicked an ordinary result on 8% of visits to a results page with a summary and on 15% without one, and ended their browsing session on 26% against 16%. Pew's comparison isn't a controlled experiment, because the searches that draw a summary tend to be longer and phrased as questions. Click-through rate (CTR) is the share of people shown a result who click it. Across more than 2,000 websites in DataForSEO's panel, the desktop CTR of the first organic result fell from 17.83% in September 2025 to 7.43% in August 2026, a drop of 58%. Mobile fell from 25.17% to 20.80%, a drop of 17%. Desktop plunged from 15.18% to 7.94% between February and March 2026. AI Overviews can't easily explain that step since the report has them almost flat that month, at 42.79% and then 43.38%. A change in the format or quality of the Overviews might explain part of it, though that is speculation on our part. Mobile, meanwhile, sank to 12.96% and then rebounded to 22.49% by May. The report itself says its data "does not establish" whether AI answers, layout changes, or changes in behavior are the cause.
