NYT Cooking’s AI assistant saved my weeknight chicken, but almost ruined my cake
I tested the new AI-powered tool on ingredient swaps, recipe scaling, and a missing Bundt pan. Around 7PM each weeknight, I find myself standing in front of the fridge, trying to decide on something for dinner that won’t make my husband complain, “Chicken again ?” Yes, I should meal plan more, but I don’t, so this is where I’m at. A nightly battle in which the chicken usually wins. Recently, my best weapon in this fight has been AI. Between Alexa Plus in the Echo smart display on my kitchen counter, the Samsung Food app on my fridge, and even an AI-powered cooking robot , AI has become my go-to sous chef — albeit one I need to keep a close eye on. It’s helped me swap ingredients, generate ideas, fix things when I mess up, and yes, even cook better. So I was intrigued when my go-to recipe app, NYT Cooking, introduced an AI assistant . Many, including some NYT Cooking users and recipe purists , are skeptical of this new tool (available to subscribers on iOS only at launch), and I get why. One of the things I like most about NYT Cooking is that it features thousands of recipes from dozens of reputable sources — such as the New York Times archives, restaurants, cookbooks, and food columnists — and real people have tested them. (I’ve tried some purely AI-generated recipes and they were not good). Another great feature of the service, which I’ve been paying for for several years, is the reader comments. Here, I’ve often found solutions to cooking dilemmas, like what to substitute for makrut lime leaves — there’s always one slightly bougie ingredient in an NYT Cooking recipe that I don’t have on hand. However, reading through hundreds of comments to find answers at 7PM on a weeknight isn’t always practical. And this is where I’ve found the NYT Cooking assistant helpful. Among other things, it was trained on user comments , and in my few days testing it, it provided practical substitutions and solid cooking guidance for the new ingredients. It also won’t unquestionably agree to crazy requests (I tried!). As with all AI tools, it’s not perfect — it almost made me put half the required butter in my cake — but as a tool, it is useful and feels like a solid application of AI. Importantly, it doesn’t change the existing recipe; it leaves it as is and creates a new one for you. It doesn’t change the existing recipe; it leaves it as is and creates a new one for you The assistant appears in the app in a small text box on the recipe page with a prompt: “Customize this recipe…” From here, you type in a question to adapt it to your needs. This can be as simple as “What can I substitute for this ingredient?” to “Scale this recipe to feed seven people, and make it vegan and gluten-free.” The New York Times has also said the feature can suggest substitutions for cooking equipment and convert measurements; the latter option has been in the app for a couple of years. Last Friday night, instead of staring aimlessly into the fridge, I turned to the Chicken folder in my NYT Cooking app Recipe Box and found a One-Pot Chicken and Rice With Ginger I’d been wanting to try. Only I didn’t have chicken thighs or half the ingredients. I could have figured these steps myself, but it was nice to have validation that my modifications wouldn’t make the dish taste terrible. I typed my dilemma into the assistant box on the recipe page: “I have chicken tenders, fresh ginger, garlic, rice, broth, limes, but no chard or cilantro or scallions. I do have green beans and red onions.” Within seconds, it created a new version of the recipe, with a new title: One-Pot Chicken and Rice With Ginger and Green Beans. It suggested a shorter browning time for the tenders, cooking the red onion alongside the chicken, and steaming the green beans on top of the rice for the last eight minutes instead of the chard. Every change was highlighted in the new recipe under a Your Version tab, and I could flip back to the original at any point. I noticed it hadn’t adjusted for the lack of cilantro, saying it was optional. So I asked if I could use Thai basil, which I happen to have, and it agreed, highlighting how it would change the flavor. While I could have figured out a lot of these steps myself, maybe with the help of Google, it was nice to have validation that my modifications wouldn’t make the dish taste terrible and nice not to have to swipe out of the app. The NYT Cooking editors say it’s been trained not to comply with requests that will taste bad . The resulting dish — which took the same amount of time to cook as the original — was delicious and got my husband’s seal of approval (he just had the leftovers for lunch). It was very different from the recipe Yasmin Fahr wrote, and I can see the purists’ point. But the rewritten recipe was much easier to use and produced a better dish than winging it with my own subs gleaned from the comments section, which I usually do with varying degrees of success. I cook regularly, so I had a sense of what ingredients I had on hand that might work for the dish I wanted to make. But the assistant can also help if you have no idea what to use as a substitute. For example, I was cooking a Thai red curry and asked what I could use instead of makrut lime leaf. It replied, saying that grated lime zest would work, but “it misses the deeper floral, herbal aroma” of fresh limes. (See? Bougie.) Over the weekend, I tried several more swaps, asking whether half-and-half would work instead of cream in a caramel apple Dutch baby, or if Castelvetrano olives and smoked paprika could stand in for kalamata and chile pepper in a roasted halibut dish. Swapping spices is always a bold move in any dish, as it can really ruin the flavor.
